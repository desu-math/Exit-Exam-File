# 📖 2. Introduction to Mathematical Modeling

### ❓ What is mathematical modeling, and why do we need it?

Real-world situations are often complicated. They involve many variables, relationships, and uncertainties.

For example:
- How will a country's population change over the next 20 years?
- How much money will an investment generate after 10 years?
- How does the price of a product affect its demand?
- How will an infectious disease spread through a population?

Mathematics provides tools for representing and analyzing such situations.

**Mathematical modeling** is the process of representing a real-world situation using mathematical concepts, relationships, equations, or structures to understand, analyze, predict, or solve problems associated with that situation.

💡 **Central idea:** Mathematical modeling connects mathematics with reality by translating real-world problems into mathematical problems.

### Example: Bank Account Growth

Suppose a person deposits 10,000 birr into a bank account that pays 10% annual simple interest.

The amount after `t` years is:

```text
A(t) = 10,000(1 + 0.1t)
```

Where:
- `A(t)` = account balance after `t` years.
- `10,000` = initial deposit.
- `0.1` = annual interest rate.
- `t` = time in years.

This equation represents the growth of money in a bank account.

Therefore, it is a mathematical model.

# 🌍 3. Reality vs. Model

### ❓ Is a mathematical model the same as the real-world situation it represents?

No. A model is a simplified representation of reality, not reality itself.

### 3.1 Reality

Reality refers to the actual world and the phenomena that exist in it.

Examples:
- Population growth.
- Vehicle movement.
- Disease transmission.
- Financial markets.
- Weather conditions.

Reality is usually complicated because many factors interact with one another.

### 3.2 Model

A model is a simplified representation of some aspect of reality.

For example, consider traffic movement. Actual traffic depends on road conditions, weather, accidents, driver behavior, and many other factors.

Instead of considering everything, we might represent the relationship between traffic volume and average speed using:

```text
v = 60 - 0.5n
```

Where:
- `v` = average speed.
- `n` = number of vehicles per hour.

This equation is a simplified representation of the traffic situation.

### Comparison

| Reality | Model |
|---|---|
| Actual situation | Representation of an actual situation |
| Usually complicated | Usually simplified |
| Contains many interacting factors | Includes selected relevant factors |
| Exists independently of our representation | Constructed by people |
| Cannot always be studied completely | Can be studied mathematically |

💡 **Remember:** A model represents selected aspects of reality that are relevant to a particular purpose.

# 🧮 4. Mathematical Model vs. Mathematical Modeling

### ❓ What is the difference between a mathematical model and mathematical modeling?

Although closely related, these terms describe different things.

### 4.1 Mathematical Model

A mathematical model is the mathematical representation of a real-world situation.

It can take different forms:

- Equations and functions.
- Graphs.
- Matrices.
- Inequalities.
- Differential equations.
- Probability distributions.

**Example:**

```text
P(t) = P₀e^(rt)
```

This equation represents exponential population growth.

It is a mathematical model.

### 4.2 Mathematical Modeling

Mathematical modeling is the process of developing, analyzing, testing, and using a mathematical model to investigate a real-world situation.

For example, developing a population growth model involves:

1. Identifying the population problem.
2. Collecting relevant information.
3. Making assumptions.
4. Constructing a mathematical equation.
5. Analyzing the equation.
6. Comparing predictions with actual population data.
7. Improving the model when necessary.

These activities constitute mathematical modeling.

### Comparison

| Mathematical Model | Mathematical Modeling |
|---|---|
| Mathematical representation itself | Process of constructing and using the representation |
| A product | A process |
| Example: `P(t) = P₀e^(rt)` | Developing and testing a population growth equation |

💡 **Remember:** A mathematical model is what we construct; mathematical modeling is how we construct and use it.

# ⭐ 5. Properties of a Mathematical Model

### ❓ What makes a mathematical model good?

A good mathematical model should possess several important properties.

### 5.1 Simplicity

A model should be as simple as possible while still serving its intended purpose.

For example, to calculate the distance traveled by a car at constant speed, we only need:

```text
d = vt
```

We do not need information about the car's color or manufacturer.

### 5.2 Accuracy

A model should represent the important characteristics of reality reasonably well.

For example, a model predicting a bank balance of 15,050 birr when the actual balance is 15,000 birr is relatively accurate.

### 5.3 Validity

A model should be suitable for the purpose for which it was developed.

For example, a simple population growth model may be suitable for short-term predictions but unsuitable for long-term predictions if migration and birth rates change significantly.

### 5.4 Reliability

A reliable model should produce consistent and reasonable results under similar conditions.

### 5.5 Flexibility

A model should be capable of being modified when new information becomes available or when the real-world situation changes.

### 5.6 Predictive Ability

A model should help estimate possible future outcomes.

Examples include:
- Predicting future population.
- Estimating business profits.
- Forecasting financial values.
- Predicting disease transmission.

**Summary:**

A good mathematical model should generally be:

1. Simple.
2. Accurate.
3. Valid.
4. Reliable.
5. Flexible.
6. Capable of providing useful predictions.

⚠️ **Important:** These properties may conflict. For example, increasing the complexity of a model may improve its accuracy but reduce its simplicity.

# 🌐 6. Three Parts of the Real World Concerning a Model

### ❓ Why don't we include everything in reality when constructing a model?

Because modeling involves simplifying reality. We must identify which parts of the real world are relevant to our investigation.

Consider a model of money growth in a bank account.

We can distinguish three parts:

### 6.1 System Under Investigation

This is the particular part of reality that we want to study.

**Example:** The bank account and the growth of deposited money.

### 6.2 Environment

This consists of external factors that may influence the system.

**Example:**
- Interest rates.
- Banking policies.
- Inflation.
- Economic conditions.

### 6.3 Irrelevant Surroundings

These are aspects of reality that exist but are not important for the particular modeling purpose.

**Example:**
- The color of the bank building.
- The number of chairs inside the bank.
- The appearance of bank employees.

### Summary

| Part | Meaning | Banking Example |
|---|---|---|
| System | What we want to investigate | Bank account |
| Environment | External factors affecting the system | Interest rate and inflation |
| Irrelevant surroundings | Factors unrelated to the investigation | Bank building color |

💡 **Remember:** Identifying these parts helps establish the boundaries and scope of a mathematical model.

# 🚀 7. Importance of Mathematical Modeling

### ❓ Why is mathematical modeling useful in real life?

Mathematical modeling provides several important advantages.

### 7.1 Understanding Real-World Phenomena

Models help us understand how different factors interact.

Example: A population growth model helps explain the relationship between population size and time.

### 7.2 Prediction

Models allow us to estimate future outcomes.

Example: A business can predict future revenue using historical sales data.

### 7.3 Decision-Making

Models support informed decisions.

Example: A manufacturing company can determine the production quantity that maximizes profit.

### 7.4 Problem-Solving

Modeling converts complicated real-world problems into mathematical problems that can be analyzed using mathematical techniques.

### 7.5 Saving Time and Money

Models allow us to investigate possible outcomes without always conducting expensive or dangerous real-world experiments.

Example: Engineers can mathematically investigate bridge designs before construction.

### 7.6 Understanding Relationships

Models help identify relationships between variables.

Example:

```text
Q = a - bP
```

This simple demand model represents the relationship between price (`P`) and quantity demanded (`Q`).

# 🔄 8. Steps of Mathematical Modeling Formulation

### ❓ How do we transform a real-world problem into a mathematical model?

Mathematical modeling follows a systematic process.

The exact number and names of steps may differ between textbooks, but the following framework describes the essential activities.

### Step 1: Identify the Real-World Problem

Determine what situation we want to investigate.

Example: Predicting the future value of a bank deposit.

### Step 2: Understand the Problem and Collect Information

Identify relevant information and data.

For a bank account, we need:
- Initial deposit.
- Interest rate.
- Time.
- Compounding method.

### Step 3: Make Assumptions

Simplify reality by making reasonable assumptions.

For example:
- The interest rate remains constant.
- No additional deposits are made.
- No withdrawals occur.

### Step 4: Formulate the Mathematical Model

Translate the situation into mathematical language.

For compound interest:

```text
A(t) = P(1 + r)^t
```

### Step 5: Solve or Analyze the Model

Substitute known values and perform mathematical calculations.

Suppose:

```text
P = 10,000
r = 0.10
t = 3
```

Then:

```text
A(3) = 10,000(1 + 0.10)^3

A(3) = 13,310 birr
```

### Step 6: Interpret the Results

Translate the mathematical answer into the language of the original problem.

The account is expected to contain 13,310 birr after three years.

### Step 7: Validate the Model

Check whether the model adequately represents reality.

This can involve comparing its predictions with actual observations or data.

### Step 8: Refine the Model

If the model is not sufficiently accurate, modify its assumptions, data, or mathematical relationships.

### The Modeling Cycle

```text
Real-World Problem
        |
        v
Understand and Collect Information
        |
        v
Make Assumptions
        |
        v
Formulate Mathematical Model
        |
        v
Solve or Analyze the Model
        |
        v
Interpret Results
        |
        v
Validate the Model
        |
        v
Refine if Necessary
        |
        +------> Repeat when necessary
```

💡 **Important:** Mathematical modeling is an iterative process. We may need to return to earlier steps when the model does not adequately describe reality.

# 🏗️ 9. Building a Mathematical Model

### ❓ What do we actually do when building a mathematical model?

Building a mathematical model involves translating a real-world situation into mathematical language.

The essential elements include:

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Variables | Quantities that can change |
| Parameters | Quantities that help determine the behavior of the model |
| Assumptions | Simplifying conditions accepted when constructing the model |
| Relationships | Mathematical connections between variables |
| Purpose | What we want to understand, calculate, or predict |

### Example: Taxi Fare Model

Suppose a taxi charges:
- Fixed initial fee = 50 birr.
- Additional charge = 15 birr per kilometer.

**Step 1: Identify variables**

Let:
- `x` = distance traveled in kilometers.
- `F(x)` = total taxi fare.

**Step 2: Identify parameters**

- Fixed fee = 50.
- Cost per kilometer = 15.

**Step 3: Establish the relationship**

Total fare = Fixed fee + Distance charge.

**Step 4: Construct the model**

```text
F(x) = 50 + 15x
```

**Step 5: Apply the model**

For a 10-kilometer journey:

```text
F(10) = 50 + 15(10)

F(10) = 200 birr
```

Therefore, the taxi fare is 200 birr.

💡 **Key idea:** Building a model requires identifying relevant variables, parameters, assumptions, and mathematical relationships.

# 📚 10. Types of Mathematical Models

Mathematical models can be classified according to different characteristics.

This chapter introduces three important classifications.

## 10.1 Deterministic vs. Stochastic Models

### ❓ Does a mathematical model always produce a fixed outcome?

No. Some models produce determined outcomes, while others incorporate randomness.

### A. Deterministic Model

A deterministic model does not explicitly include randomness. Its output is completely determined by its inputs and parameters.

**Example: Simple Interest**

```text
A = P(1 + rt)
```

Suppose:

```text
P = 10,000
r = 0.10
t = 2
```

Then:

```text
A = 10,000(1 + 0.10 × 2)

A = 12,000 birr
```

The same inputs always produce the same result.

### B. Stochastic Model

A stochastic model explicitly incorporates randomness or uncertainty.

**Example: Customer Arrivals**

Suppose we want to model the number of customers arriving at a bank during one hour.

We may use:

```text
X ~ Poisson(30)
```

This represents a random number of customer arrivals with an average rate of 30 customers per hour.

The actual number may be 25, 28, 32, or another possible value.

### Comparison

| Deterministic | Stochastic |
|---|---|
| Does not explicitly include randomness | Explicitly includes randomness |
| Same inputs produce the same output | Outcomes may vary |
| Produces determined outcomes | Produces probabilistic outcomes |
| Example: Simple interest | Example: Random customer arrivals |

---

## 10.2 Continuous vs. Discrete Models

### ❓ Can the variables in a mathematical model take any value, or must they take separate values?

This distinction concerns the nature of the quantities being modeled.

### A. Continuous Model

A continuous model describes quantities that can take any value within a given interval.

**Examples:**
- Temperature.
- Distance.
- Time.
- Water volume.
- Human height.

Consider the distance traveled by a car at constant speed:

```text
d(t) = 60t
```

Here, time can take any nonnegative real value.

For example:

```text
d(1)   = 60 km
d(1.5) = 90 km
d(2.5) = 150 km
```

### B. Discrete Model

A discrete model describes quantities that take separate or distinct values.

**Examples:**
- Number of students.
- Number of customers.
- Number of vehicles.
- Number of bank transactions.

Suppose a school initially has 200 students and gains 10 students each year.

```text
P(n) = 200 + 10n
```

Here, `n` represents whole-number time periods.

### Comparison

| Continuous | Discrete |
|---|---|
| Variables can take continuous values | Variables take distinct values |
| Can include fractional values | Often involves countable quantities |
| Example: Temperature | Example: Number of students |
| Example: Distance | Example: Number of vehicles |

---

## 10.3 Static vs. Dynamic Models

### ❓ Does a mathematical model describe only a particular situation, or does it explain how that situation changes over time?

This distinction concerns the treatment of time.

### A. Static Model

A static model describes a situation at a particular point in time or under fixed conditions.

It does not explicitly describe how the system evolves over time.

**Example: Profit Calculation**

```text
Profit = Revenue - Cost
```

Suppose:

```text
Revenue = 50,000 birr
Cost = 30,000 birr
```

Then:

```text
Profit = 20,000 birr
```

This calculates profit for a particular situation.

### B. Dynamic Model

A dynamic model describes how a system changes or evolves over time.

**Example: Population Growth**

```text
P(t) = P₀(1 + r)^t
```

Suppose:

```text
P₀ = 1,000
r = 0.05
```

Then:

```text
P(t) = 1,000(1.05)^t
```

The model describes how population changes over successive years.

### Comparison

| Static | Dynamic |
|---|---|
| Describes a situation under fixed conditions | Describes changes over time |
| Does not explicitly represent temporal evolution | Explicitly represents temporal evolution |
| Example: Current profit calculation | Example: Future profit prediction |
| Focuses on a particular state | Focuses on changing states |

### Summary of the Three Classifications

| Classification | First Type | Second Type |
|---|---|---|
| Randomness | Deterministic | Stochastic |
| Nature of quantities | Continuous | Discrete |
| Time | Static | Dynamic |

⚠️ **Important:** These classifications describe different characteristics of a model. A model can be deterministic, continuous, and dynamic at the same time.

For example:

```text
d(t) = 60t
```

This vehicle-motion model is:
- Deterministic.
- Continuous.
- Dynamic.

# 💡 11. Examples of Mathematical Models

The following examples connect mathematical modeling concepts with different real-world applications.

### Example 1: Simple Interest Model

```text
A(t) = P(1 + rt)
```

Where:
- `P` = initial principal.
- `r` = annual interest rate.
- `t` = time.
- `A(t)` = accumulated amount.

**Application:** Calculating the future value of an investment earning simple interest.

**Classification:**
- Deterministic.
- Continuous or discrete depending on the time representation.
- Dynamic.

### Example 2: Exponential Population Growth

```text
P(t) = P₀e^(rt)
```

Where:
- `P₀` = initial population.
- `r` = continuous growth rate.
- `t` = time.

**Application:** Predicting population growth under a constant proportional growth rate.

**Classification:**
- Deterministic.
- Continuous.
- Dynamic.

### Example 3: Demand Model

```text
Q = a - bP
```

Where:
- `Q` = quantity demanded.
- `P` = price.
- `a, b` = constants.

**Application:** Studying the relationship between product price and quantity demanded.

**Classification:**
- Deterministic.
- Continuous if price and quantity are treated as continuous variables.
- Static under fixed market conditions.

### Example 4: Customer Arrival Model

```text
X ~ Poisson(λ)
```

Where:
- `X` = number of customer arrivals during a specified period.
- `λ` = average arrival rate for that period.

**Application:** Modeling customer arrivals at banks, hospitals, and supermarkets.

**Classification:**
- Stochastic.
- Discrete.
- Can be part of a dynamic model when arrivals are studied over time.

### Example 5: Vehicle Motion Model

```text
d(t) = vt
```

Where:
- `d(t)` = distance traveled.
- `v` = constant speed.
- `t` = time.

**Application:** Calculating the distance traveled by a vehicle.

**Classification:**
- Deterministic.
- Continuous.
- Dynamic.

### Summary of Examples

| Model | Deterministic/Stochastic | Continuous/Discrete | Static/Dynamic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple interest | Deterministic | Depends on time representation | Dynamic |
| Population growth | Deterministic | Continuous | Dynamic |
| Demand model | Deterministic | Depends on variables | Static |
| Customer arrivals | Stochastic | Discrete | Can be dynamic |
| Vehicle motion | Deterministic | Continuous | Dynamic |

They require understanding the situation, selecting relevant information, constructing assumptions, and developing suitable mathematical relationships.

💡 **Remember:** Mathematical modeling prepares us to use mathematics as a practical tool for understanding and solving real-world problems.

# ⚠️ 13. Common Mistakes and Important Distinctions

| Common Mistake | Correct Understanding |
|---|---|
| Thinking that a model is reality itself | A model is a simplified representation of reality. |
| Confusing mathematical modeling with a mathematical model | A model is a product; modeling is a process. |
| Thinking every model must be accurate in every situation | A model is valid only within its intended purpose and assumptions. |
| Assuming deterministic means accurate | Deterministic means the output is determined by the inputs and parameters. |
| Confusing stochastic with discrete | Stochastic concerns randomness; discrete concerns distinct values. |
| Confusing continuous with dynamic | Continuous concerns the nature of quantities; dynamic concerns changes over time. |
| Thinking every model must be complicated | Simple models can be very useful. |
| Assuming a model never needs modification | Models may need refinement when new information becomes available. |

### Three Important Questions for Model Classification

Whenever you are asked to classify a mathematical model, ask:

1. **Does it explicitly incorporate randomness?**
   - No → Deterministic.
   - Yes → Stochastic.

2. **What is the nature of its quantities?**
   - Continuous values → Continuous.
   - Distinct values → Discrete.

3. **Does it explicitly describe changes over time?**
   - No → Static.
   - Yes → Dynamic.

These questions make model classification much easier.

A business can use mathematical modeling by:

1. Collecting historical revenue and cost data.
2. Identifying important variables and parameters.
3. Making reasonable assumptions.
4. Constructing mathematical relationships between revenue, cost, and profit.
5. Analyzing the model to predict future profit.
6. Comparing predictions with actual business results.

# 🎯 17. Conclusion

Mathematical modeling is the process of connecting mathematics with real-world situations.

The central idea is:

**Observe reality → Simplify the situation → Construct a mathematical model → Analyze the model → Interpret the results → Validate and improve the model.**

We have also learned that mathematical models can be classified according to three independent characteristics:

1. Deterministic or stochastic.
2. Continuous or discrete.
3. Static or dynamic.

The most important lesson from this chapter is that mathematical modeling is not simply about solving equations. It is about understanding real-world problems and discovering suitable mathematical relationships to represent them.

> 💡 Mathematics provides the tools, while mathematical modeling teaches us how to apply those tools to reality.

# Cyclic Groups

1. Theorem: Every cyclic group is Abelian

Remember:

A **cyclic group** is a group whose elements can all be generated by taking powers of one element.
An **Abelian group** is a group in which the operation is commutative: xy = yx.

---

## Why is every cyclic group Abelian?

Suppose G is a cyclic group generated by g. This means every element of G can be written as a power of g.

For any two elements x and y, we can write:

x = gⁿ
y = gᵐ

Here, n and m are integers.

Now calculate:

xy = gⁿgᵐ = gⁿ⁺ᵐ

Since adding integers is commutative, n + m = m + n. Therefore,

xy = gⁿ⁺ᵐ = gᵐ⁺ⁿ = gᵐgⁿ = yx

Thus, xy = yx, so G is Abelian.

---

## What should you remember for the exam?

Every cyclic group is Abelian, but not every Abelian group is cyclic.

For example, the Klein four group is Abelian but not cyclic.

2. The Division Algorithm for Integers

We need to introduce the division algorithm because it is used to prove that every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

Statement

For any integer n and positive integer m, there exist unique integers q and r such that:

n = mq + r, where 0 ≤ r < m

Here:

n = the integer being divided
m = the positive divisor
q = the quotient
r = the remainder

---

Important: The remainder must be nonnegative, even when n is negative.

---

Example 1: Divide 37 by 7

We have:

37 = 7 × 5 + 2

Therefore:

q = 5
r = 2

The remainder satisfies 0 ≤ 2 < 7.

Example 2: Divide −38 by 7

We need a nonnegative remainder smaller than 7.

−38 = 7 × (−6) + 4

Therefore:

q = −6
r = 4

Notice that −38 = 7 × (−5) − 3 is not in the required form because the remainder would be negative.

---

Exam tip: When dividing a negative integer, make sure the remainder satisfies 0 ≤ r < m.

3. Theorem: Every Subgroup of a Cyclic Group Is Cyclic

In simple terms, if a group can be generated by one element, then any subgroup inside it can also be generated by one element.


Let's unpack this statement for further simplification..

Recall that a group G is cyclic if there is an element g in G such that every element of G can be obtained by taking integer powers of g.

In symbols:

G = ⟨g⟩ = {gⁿ : n ∈ Z}

Here, ⟨g⟩ means the subgroup generated by g.

The theorem tells us that if we take any subgroup H of a cyclic group G, then H must also have a single element that generates all its elements.

2. A simple example

Consider the additive group of integers:

G = (Z, +)

This is a cyclic group because every integer can be generated by repeatedly adding 1 or −1.

For example:

Z = ⟨1⟩

Now consider the subgroup of even integers:

H = { ..., −6, −4, −2, 0, 2, 4, 6, ... }

Notice that every element of H can be obtained by repeatedly adding 2 or −2.

Therefore:

H = ⟨2⟩

So H is cyclic, too!

The theorem says this is not just true for the even integers. Every subgroup of every cyclic group is cyclic.

The subgroups of Z under addition are precisely the sets nZ, where n is an integer.

The notation nZ means the set of all integer multiples of n.

In other words,

nZ = {nk : k ∈ Z}.

For example:

Example 1: 2Z

5Z = { ..., −10, −5, 0, 5, 10, 15, ... }

Therefore,

Every integer is a multiple of 1. Therefore,

1Z = Z.

Similarly, (−1)Z = Z.

Example 5: The subgroup containing only zero

If n = 0, then

0Z = {0}.

This is also a subgroup of Z.

3. Integer combinations and the greatest common divisor

Your PDF next introduces sets of the form

H = {nr + ms : n, m ∈ Z}.

Here, r and s are fixed integers, while n and m can be any integers.

This set consists of all integer combinations of r and s.

The PDF asks us to prove that this set is a subgroup of Z and then apply the result to a particular example.

3.1 What is an integer combination?

Suppose r = 8 and s = 14.

An integer combination of 8 and 14 has the form

8n + 14m,

where n and m are integers.

Some examples are:

8(1) + 14(0) = 8
8(0) + 14(1) = 14
8(2) + 14(−1) = 2
8(−1) + 14(1) = 6
8(1) + 14(−1) = −6

The set contains every value obtainable in this way.

Define

H = {8n + 14m : n, m ∈ Z}.

3.2 Why is H a subgroup of Z?

We can use the subgroup test for additive groups.

A nonempty subset H of Z is a subgroup if, whenever a and b belong to H, the difference a − b also belongs to H.

First: H is nonempty.

Choose n = 0 and m = 0.

Then

8(0) + 14(0) = 0.

Therefore, 0 belongs to H.

Second: H is closed under differences.

Suppose

a = 8n₁ + 14m₁

and

b = 8n₂ + 14m₂.

Then

a − b
= (8n₁ + 14m₁) − (8n₂ + 14m₂)
= 8(n₁ − n₂) + 14(m₁ − m₂).

Since n₁ − n₂ and m₁ − m₂ are integers, a − b also belongs to H.

Therefore, by the subgroup test,

H is a subgroup of Z.

3.3 What is its generator?

The PDF asks:

Let H = {8n + 14m : n, m ∈ Z}. What is the generator of H?

We know that every subgroup of Z has the form dZ for some nonnegative integer d.

In this case, the generator is the greatest common divisor of 8 and 14.

The greatest common divisor is the largest positive integer that divides both numbers.

The positive divisors of 8 are 1, 2, 4, 8.

The positive divisors of 14 are 1, 2, 7, 14.

Their greatest common divisor is

gcd(8, 14) = 2.

Notice that 2 can actually be obtained as an integer combination:

2 = 2(8) − 1(14).

Thus, 2 belongs to H.

Every integer combination of 8 and 14 is also a multiple of 2. Conversely, because 2 belongs to H, every integer multiple of 2 belongs to H.

Consequently,

H = 2Z = ⟨2⟩.

General result

For integers r and s, not both zero,

{nr + ms : n, m ∈ Z} = gcd(r, s)Z.

This result connects group theory with the greatest common divisor.

It also explains why the PDF's example has generator 2.

4. The structure of cyclic groups: Isomorphism

Your PDF next explains that all cyclic groups can be described, up to isomorphism, using two familiar groups:

The additive group of integers, Z.
The additive group of integers modulo n, Zₙ.

To understand the theorem, we first need to understand the idea of an isomorphism.

4.1 What does isomorphism mean?

Two groups are isomorphic when they have the same algebraic structure, even if their elements are named differently.

An isomorphism is a function between two groups that:

Is one-to-one.
Is onto.
Preserves the group operation.

Suppose φ maps a group G to a group H.

Preserving the operation means

φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b)

for all a and b in G.

For additive groups, the equivalent condition is

φ(a + b) = φ(a) + φ(b).

If an isomorphism exists between G and H, we write

G ≅ H.

The groups may use different symbols, but their group structures are equivalent.

4.2 Every infinite cyclic group is isomorphic to Z

Theorem: If G is an infinite cyclic group generated by g, then G ≅ Z.

Suppose

G = ⟨g⟩.

Because G is cyclic, every element of G is a power of g:

G = {gⁿ : n ∈ Z}.

Define a function

φ : Z → G

by

φ(n) = gⁿ.

In words, the integer n is mapped to the element gⁿ.

For example:

0 maps to e.
1 maps to g.
2 maps to g².
−1 maps to g⁻¹.

Now let's understand why this function is an isomorphism.

First: It is one-to-one.

Suppose

φ(n) = φ(m).

Then

gⁿ = gᵐ.

This implies

gⁿ⁻ᵐ = e.

Because G is infinite cyclic, no nonzero integer power of g equals the identity. Therefore, n − m = 0, which gives

n = m.

Hence, φ is one-to-one.

Second: It is onto.

Every element x in G has the form gⁿ for some integer n.

But φ(n) = gⁿ.

Therefore, every element of G is the image of some integer, so φ is onto.

Third: It preserves the operation.

For any integers n and m,

φ(n + m) = gⁿ⁺ᵐ.

By the exponent law,

gⁿ⁺ᵐ = gⁿgᵐ.

Therefore,

φ(n + m) = φ(n)φ(m).

Thus, φ preserves the group operation.

Since φ is one-to-one, onto, and operation-preserving, it is an isomorphism.

Hence,

G ≅ Z.

What does this result tell us?

Every infinite cyclic group has the same structure as the integers under addition.

The elements may look different, and the operation may be written differently, but their algebraic structures are equivalent.

---

4.3 Every finite cyclic group of order n is isomorphic to Zₙ

The PDF's second result is:

If G is cyclic and has n elements, then G ≅ Zₙ.

Suppose

G = ⟨g⟩ and |G| = n.

Because G has n elements, its elements can be written as

G = {e, g, g², ..., gⁿ⁻¹}.

These elements are distinct, and gⁿ = e.

The group Zₙ consists of the residue classes represented by

{0, 1, 2, ..., n − 1},

with addition modulo n.

Define a map

ψ : G → Zₙ

by

ψ(gⁱ) = i mod n,

for i = 0, 1, ..., n − 1.

In simple terms:

e maps to 0.
g maps to 1.
g² maps to 2.
...
gⁿ⁻¹ maps to n − 1.

This function is well-defined, one-to-one, onto, and preserves the operation. Thus, it is an isomorphism.

Therefore,

G ≅ Zₙ.

Example

Suppose a cyclic group has six elements and is generated by g.

Its elements are

G = {e, g, g², g³, g⁴, g⁵}.

Its structure corresponds to Z₆:

Z₆ = {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5},

with addition modulo 6.

The correspondence is:

Cyclic group G	Z₆
e	0
g	1
g²	2
g³	3
g⁴	4
g⁵	5

For example, g⁴ · g³ = g⁷ = g, because g⁶ = e.

The corresponding calculation in Z₆ is

4 + 3 = 7 ≡ 1 (mod 6).

Both groups follow the same structural rule.

Main conclusion

Every cyclic group is structurally equivalent to either:

Z, if it is infinite.
Zₙ, if it is finite with n elements.

This is one of the main results of the section.

5. Subgroups of finite cyclic groups

Now your PDF returns to the subgroups of cyclic groups and establishes a formula for the number of elements in a subgroup generated by a particular element.

5.1 The theorem

Suppose G is a cyclic group such that

G = ⟨g⟩ and |G| = n.

Let

b = gˢ.

Define

H = ⟨b⟩.

Then the number of elements in H is

|H| = n / gcd(n, s).

Here, gcd(n, s) means the greatest common divisor of n and s.

Let's apply the formula to the examples from your PDF.

Example 1: Find ⟨3⟩ in Z₁₂

Consider Z₁₂ under addition modulo 12.

The group has 12 elements.

We want to find the subgroup generated by 3.

The formula gives

|⟨3⟩| = 12 / gcd(12, 3).

Since gcd(12, 3) = 3,

|⟨3⟩| = 12 / 3 = 4.

To identify the elements, repeatedly add 3 modulo 12:

Start with 0, the identity.
0 + 3 = 3.
3 + 3 = 6.
6 + 3 = 9.
9 + 3 = 12 ≡ 0 (mod 12).

We have returned to zero.

Therefore,

⟨3⟩ = {0, 3, 6, 9}.

It contains four elements.

Example 2: Find ⟨8⟩ in Z₁₂

Apply the formula:

|⟨8⟩| = 12 / gcd(12, 8).

Since gcd(12, 8) = 4,

|⟨8⟩| = 12 / 4 = 3.

Now generate the subgroup:

Start at 0.
0 + 8 = 8.
8 + 8 = 16 ≡ 4 (mod 12).
4 + 8 = 12 ≡ 0 (mod 12).

Therefore,

⟨8⟩ = {0, 4, 8}.

It contains three elements.

Notice that although we generate the subgroup using 8, its elements are 0, 4, and 8.

Example 3: Find ⟨5⟩ in Z₁₂

Apply the formula:

|⟨5⟩| = 12 / gcd(12, 5).

Since gcd(12, 5) = 1,

|⟨5⟩| = 12 / 1 = 12.

Therefore, ⟨5⟩ contains all 12 elements of Z₁₂.

Hence,

⟨5⟩ = Z₁₂.

This means 5 is a generator of Z₁₂.

What pattern do we observe?

Compare the three examples:

Element	gcd(element, 12)	Subgroup size	Result
3	3	12 / 3 = 4	Proper subgroup
8	4	12 / 4 = 3	Proper subgroup
5	1	12 / 1 = 12	Entire group

The greatest common divisor helps determine how many elements are generated.

If gcd(s, n) = 1, then gˢ generates the entire cyclic group of order n.

That leads directly to the next result.

6. Other generators of a cyclic group

Your PDF gives the following corollary:

If g is a generator of a cyclic group of order n, then the other generators are the elements gʳ for which gcd(r, n) = 1.

Let's understand it.

Suppose

G = ⟨g⟩ and |G| = n.

We want to know which powers of g also generate G.

Consider gʳ.

The subgroup generated by gʳ has

n / gcd(n, r)

elements.

For gʳ to generate the whole group, this subgroup must have n elements.

Therefore,

n / gcd(n, r) = n.

This is possible exactly when

gcd(n, r) = 1.

So an element gʳ is a generator precisely when r and n are relatively prime.

Two integers are relatively prime if their greatest common divisor is 1.

Example: Generators of Z₁₂

The group Z₁₂ is generated by 1.

An element r generates Z₁₂ if

gcd(r, 12) = 1.

Consider the integers from 1 to 11:

r	gcd(r, 12)	Does r generate Z₁₂?
1	1	Yes
2	2	No
3	3	No
4	4	No
5	1	Yes
6	6	No
7	1	Yes
8	4	No
9	3	No
10	2	No
11	1	Yes

Therefore, the generators of Z₁₂ are

1, 5, 7, and 11.

Equivalently,

Z₁₂ = ⟨1⟩ = ⟨5⟩ = ⟨7⟩ = ⟨11⟩.

Exercise from your PDF: Generators of Z₁₈

The PDF asks you to list all generators of Z₁₈.

The criterion is

gcd(r, 18) = 1.

The integers from 1 to 17 that satisfy this condition are

1, 5, 7, 11, 13, and 17.

Therefore, these are the generators of Z₁₈.

For example, 5 generates Z₁₈ because gcd(5, 18) = 1, whereas 6 does not because gcd(6, 18) = 6.

7. Generating sets

So far, we have studied a single element generating a group or subgroup.

Your PDF now generalizes the idea to a set of elements generating a group.

7.1 Definition

Let G be a group, and let X be a subset of G.

The subgroup generated by X, denoted by ⟨X⟩, is the smallest subgroup of G that contains every element of X.

If

⟨X⟩ = G,

then X is called a generating set for G.

The difference is:

⟨X⟩ is the subgroup generated by X.
X is a generating set for G when ⟨X⟩ = G.

A generating set can contain more than one element.

7.2 Why can multiple elements be useful?

For a single element a, the subgroup ⟨a⟩ contains all integer powers of a.

For two elements a and b, a subgroup containing both must contain:

All powers of a.
All powers of b.
Products of these powers.
Inverses of these elements.

For example, it must contain elements of the form

aⁿbᵐ,

for integers n and m.

The subgroup generated by {a, b} is the smallest subgroup containing both a and b.

7.3 Example: The Klein four group

Your PDF considers the Klein four group:

V = {e, a, b, c}.

Its elements satisfy

a² = b² = c² = e,

and the product ab equals c.

Therefore, if a subgroup contains a and b, it must also contain ab = c.

It must also contain e, the identity.

Thus, a subgroup containing both a and b contains all four elements:

{e, a, b, c}.

Consequently,

⟨a, b⟩ = V.

So {a, b} is a generating set for V.

Similarly, the PDF states that V is also generated by

{a, c}
{b, c}
{a, b, c}

The first two sets work for the same reason: the product of the two nonidentity elements gives the remaining nonidentity element.

7.4 Example: Generating Z₆ using {2, 3}

Your PDF also gives an example involving Z₆.

Under addition modulo 6,

Z₆ = {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5}.

The PDF says that Z₆ is generated by {2, 3}.

Why?

A subgroup containing 2 and 3 must contain their sum:

2 + 3 = 5 (mod 6).

It must also contain 2 + 2 = 4 (mod 6), and then 2 + 4 = 0 (mod 6).

Since it contains 3 and 2, it contains every element of Z₆.

Therefore,

⟨{2, 3}⟩ = Z₆.

Notice that neither 2 nor 3 generates Z₆ individually:

⟨2⟩ = {0, 2, 4}.
⟨3⟩ = {0, 3}.

But together, they generate the entire group.

The PDF also gives {3, 4} and {2, 3, 4} as other generating sets.

Important distinction

A group can have many different generating sets.

For Z₆, both {1} and {5} generate the group individually. The set {2, 3} also generates the group, even though neither 2 nor 3 does so alone.

8. The intersection of subgroups is a subgroup

Your PDF next establishes a theorem about intersections.

Theorem: The intersection of any family of subgroups of G is a subgroup of G.

Let's understand what an intersection means.

Suppose H₁ and H₂ are subgroups of G.

Their intersection is

H₁ ∩ H₂.

It contains exactly those elements that belong to both H₁ and H₂.

For example, in Z under addition, consider

H₁ = 2Z

and

H₂ = 3Z.

Then:

H₁ contains all even integers.
H₂ contains all multiples of 3.

Their intersection contains integers that are multiples of both 2 and 3.

Therefore,

H₁ ∩ H₂ = 6Z.

For example, 6 and 12 belong to both groups, but 2 belongs only to H₁, while 3 belongs only to H₂.

8.1 Proof of the theorem

Suppose {Hᵢ : i ∈ I} is a family of subgroups of G.

Let

K = ⋂ Hᵢ.

We want to show that K is a subgroup of G.

We use the subgroup test: K must be nonempty and must contain xy⁻¹ whenever x and y belong to K.

Step 1: Take x and y in K.

Because x and y belong to the intersection, they belong to every subgroup Hᵢ.

Thus,

x, y ∈ Hᵢ for every i ∈ I.

Step 2: Use the subgroup property of each Hᵢ.

Each Hᵢ is a subgroup, so it contains xy⁻¹.

Therefore,

xy⁻¹ ∈ Hᵢ for every i ∈ I.

Step 3: Return to the intersection.

If xy⁻¹ belongs to every Hᵢ, then it belongs to their intersection.

Thus,

xy⁻¹ ∈ K.

The intersection is nonempty because every subgroup contains the identity element of G.

Therefore, by the subgroup test,

⋂ Hᵢ is a subgroup of G.

Why is this theorem useful?

It allows us to construct new subgroups by intersecting existing subgroups.

For example, the intersection 2Z ∩ 3Z gives 6Z, another subgroup of Z.

The theorem works for any family of subgroups, not just two.

9. The subgroup generated by a subset

The last major concept in Point 5 is the subgroup generated by a subset, which combines the ideas of generating sets and intersections.

9.1 Definition

Let G be a group, and let X be a subset of G.

The subgroup generated by X, written ⟨X⟩, is the smallest subgroup of G containing X.

Your PDF also describes it as the intersection of all subgroups containing X.

In symbols,

⟨X⟩ = ⋂ {H ≤ G : X ⊆ H}.

Let's unpack this expression.

We consider all subgroups H of G that contain every element of X. We then take their intersection.

Since the intersection of subgroups is a subgroup, the resulting set is itself a subgroup.

It contains X, and it is contained in every subgroup that contains X. Therefore, it is the smallest such subgroup.

9.2 Example from your PDF: Z₁₄ and {2, 4, 8}

The PDF asks us to find the subgroup generated by {2, 4, 8} in Z₁₄.

Remember that Z₁₄ uses addition modulo 14.

We want the smallest subgroup containing 2, 4, and 8.

Because the subgroup contains 2, it must contain all multiples of 2 modulo 14:

⟨2⟩ = {0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12}.

Notice that 4 and 8 are already in this subgroup.

Therefore, ⟨2⟩ contains all three elements 2, 4, and 8.

Furthermore, any subgroup containing {2, 4, 8} must contain 2 and therefore must contain all of ⟨2⟩.

Thus,

⟨{2, 4, 8}⟩ = ⟨2⟩.

This agrees with the PDF's solution, which uses

gcd(2, 4, 8) = 2.

9.3 Example: Z₁₄ and {2, 5, 6}

The PDF also asks about the set {2, 5, 6} in Z₁₄.

The subgroup generated by these elements must contain 5.

Now,

gcd(5, 14) = 1.

Therefore, 5 generates all of Z₁₄.

Consequently, any subgroup of Z₁₄ containing 5 must be the whole group.

Hence,

⟨{2, 5, 6}⟩ = Z₁₄.

This is a useful shortcut: if even one element in a generating set already generates the entire group, then the set generates the entire group.

Exercise 1(a): Find the number of elements in ⟨25⟩ in Z₃₀

The order of the cyclic group Z₃₀ is 30.

The order of the subgroup generated by 25 is

|⟨25⟩| = 30 / gcd(30, 25).

Since gcd(30, 25) = 5,

|⟨25⟩| = 30 / 5 = 6.

Answer: ⟨25⟩ contains 6 elements.

Exercise 1(b): Find the number of elements in ⟨i⟩ in the nonzero complex numbers under multiplication

The element i satisfies

i³ = −i,

i⁴ = 1.

After taking four powers, we return to the identity.

Thus,

⟨i⟩ = {1, i, −1, −i}.

These four elements are distinct.

Answer: ⟨i⟩ contains 4 elements.

Exercise 2: Find all possible orders of subgroups of Z₁₅

Z₁₅ is a cyclic group of order 15.

The possible orders of its subgroups are the positive divisors of 15:

1, 3, 5, 15.

Each divisor corresponds to a subgroup.

For example:

Order 1: {0}.
Order 3: ⟨5⟩ = {0, 5, 10}.
Order 5: ⟨3⟩ = {0, 3, 6, 9, 12}.
Order 15: Z₁₅ itself.

Answer: 1, 3, 5, and 15.

Exercise 3: Show that a group with only finitely many subgroups is finite

Suppose G has only finitely many subgroups.

Every element a in G generates a cyclic subgroup ⟨a⟩. Therefore, if G has only finitely many subgroups, it has only finitely many distinct cyclic subgroups.

Now consider two possibilities.

Case 1: Every cyclic subgroup is finite.

Since there are only finitely many cyclic subgroups and each contains finitely many elements, their union is finite.

Every element of G belongs to its own cyclic subgroup, so the union of all cyclic subgroups is G.

Therefore, G is finite.

Case 2: G contains an element a of infinite order.

Then ⟨a⟩ is an infinite cyclic subgroup.

But an infinite cyclic group has infinitely many distinct subgroups:

⟨a⟩, ⟨a²⟩, ⟨a³⟩, ⟨a⁴⟩, ...

These subgroups are distinct because a has infinite order. This contradicts the assumption that G has only finitely many subgroups.

Thus, G cannot contain an element of infinite order.

Consequently, every cyclic subgroup is finite, and by Case 1, G is finite.

Hence, a group with only finitely many subgroups is finite.

Exercise 4: If p and q are distinct primes, find the number of generators of Zₚq

The order of Zₚq is pq.

An element r generates Zₚq if and only if

gcd(r, pq) = 1.

Since p and q are distinct primes, an integer from 1 to pq is relatively prime to pq precisely when it is divisible by neither p nor q.

Among the pq residue classes, there are:

pq classes in total.
q classes divisible by p.
p classes divisible by q.
One class, 0, counted in both of those sets.

By inclusion-exclusion, the number relatively prime to pq is

pq − q − p + 1.

Factorizing gives

(p − 1)(q − 1).

Therefore, Zₚq has (p − 1)(q − 1) generators.

Exercise 5: Show that Zₚ has no nontrivial subgroup when p is prime

Here, p is a prime number.

Consider any subgroup H of Zₚ.

If H = {0}, it is the trivial subgroup.

Otherwise, H contains a nonzero element a.

Because p is prime and 0 < a < p,

gcd(a, p) = 1.

By the generator criterion, a generates Zₚ.

Therefore,

⟨a⟩ = Zₚ.

Since H contains a, it must contain every element generated by a. Hence H = Zₚ.

Thus, the only subgroups are

{0} and Zₚ.

So Zₚ has no nontrivial proper subgroup.

Exercise 6: Show that H = {nr + ms : n, m ∈ Z} is a subgroup of Z

This is the general version of the integer-combination example.

Let r and s be fixed integers, and define

H = {nr + ms : n, m ∈ Z}.

We use the subgroup test for the additive group Z.

Step 1: Show H is nonempty.

Taking n = m = 0 gives

0 = 0r + 0s.

Therefore, 0 belongs to H.

Step 2: Show H is closed under differences.

Take any two elements

a = n₁r + m₁s

and

b = n₂r + m₂s.

Then

a − b
= (n₁ − n₂)r + (m₁ − m₂)s.

Because n₁ − n₂ and m₁ − m₂ are integers, a − b belongs to H.

Thus, H is nonempty and closed under differences.

By the subgroup test,

H is a subgroup of Z.

Furthermore, when r and s are not both zero, its generator is gcd(r, s).
